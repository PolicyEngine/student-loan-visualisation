Budgetary impact of student loan reforms

PolicyEngine estimates the annual fiscal cost to government of two student loan reforms. Each reform reduces government revenue in a different way:

Cancel the threshold freeze. Under current law the Plan 2 repayment threshold is frozen at £29,385 for 2027–2029. Cancelling the freeze would uprate it with RPI each year, meaning graduates repay less. The fiscal cost is the lost repayment revenue — the gap between what the government would have collected under the freeze and what it collects with a higher threshold.

Cap interest at RPI. Plan 2 loans currently accrue interest at RPI plus a margin of 0–3%, scaled by income. Capping interest at RPI removes that margin entirely, so graduates owe only what they borrowed in real terms. The fiscal cost is the lost interest income on the student loan book — the 0–3% margin that no longer accrues, reducing the value of the government’s loan asset.

Comparison with other estimates

For the threshold freeze, the OBR’s Autumn Budget 2025 policy costings estimate the freeze raises £0.3bn in 2027–28 for the exchequer. PolicyEngine estimates cancelling the freeze would cost £0.3bn in 2027–28.

For the interest rate cap, the Conservative Party estimates reported in The Times put the cost at £2bn a year for the first few years, falling to £1.3bn later. PolicyEngine estimates £1.8–2.0bn per year over 2026–2030.

Methodology

Reform 1: Cancel the threshold freeze

  1. Baseline (current law): PolicyEngine UK default — the Plan 2 repayment threshold is frozen at £29,385 for 2027–2029, then resumes uprating.
  2. Calculate RPI-uprated thresholds: Apply OBR November 2025 EFO forecasts (RPI 3.7% in 2026–27, falling to 2.9% long-run; earnings growth 3.2%, falling to 2.3%) to uprate the threshold each year from its 2026 level.
  3. Run microsimulation for both the baseline (frozen) and reformed (uprated) scenarios across the graduate population.
  4. Fiscal impact = reformed total repayments − baseline total repayments. A negative value means the government collects less.

Reform 2: Cap interest at RPI

  1. Estimate each borrower’s current Plan 2 balance (PolicyEngine UK does not track individual loan balances, so this step uses our own assumptions):
    • Start with £45,600 (DfE Student Loan Forecasts, latest cohort average), deflated by RPI for earlier cohorts.
    • Simulate year-by-year from graduation: interest accrues on the balance and repayments are deducted.
    • Uses actual historical March RPI values, including the government caps applied in 2022–23.
  2. Run microsimulation with balance-tracking variables to project interest accrual under both regimes.
  3. Compare interest accrual: baseline (RPI + 0–3% margin) vs reformed (RPI only).
  4. Fiscal cost = lost interest income on the loan book — the margin that no longer accrues, reducing the government’s loan asset value.

Sensitivity analysis (Reform 2)

The interest cap estimate depends on assumptions about borrowers’ current loan balances, which PolicyEngine UK does not track directly. We tested four alternative assumptions to check how much they move the headline estimate:

  1. RPI-deflated historical income: The baseline uses current income to estimate past repayments. In practice, graduates earned less in earlier years. This test deflates income by cumulative RPI for each historical year, resulting in lower past repayments and higher current balances.
  2. Graduation age 22: The baseline assumes graduation at 21. Shifting to 22 gives each borrower one fewer year of repayment history, increasing their estimated balance.
  3. Debt variance: The baseline gives every borrower in a cohort the same starting debt. This test applies a log-normal spread (standard deviation 0.3) around the cohort average, so some borrowers start with more and some with less. This raises the average balance because interest compounds faster on larger debts.
